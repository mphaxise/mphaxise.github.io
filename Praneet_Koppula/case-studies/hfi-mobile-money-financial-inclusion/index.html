Designing mobile money from household financial life.

At Human Factors International, I co-authored published research on how mobile money could support financial inclusion for women in bottom-of-the-pyramid households in urban India. A surviving field-evidence artifact directly connects me to the study’s research archive.

The central idea

Inclusion changes the product model.

A useful service had to fit how money, trust, obligation, access, and decision-making already worked within a household.

Research frame

Financial behavior lives inside a system.

The study connected individual transactions to household relationships, informal and formal providers, access to mobile phones, migration history, and the products available to low-income urban families.

Household roles

01 / Lives

Begin with the way money already moves.

The research centered women whose financial choices were shaped by household roles, informal practices, limited access, and migration from rural to urban India. It examined the existing transaction ecosystem before developing mobile-money opportunity areas.

This frame gave product design a social and institutional starting point. Access, trust, obligation, control, and risk all influenced whether a service could fit daily life.

02 / System

Map relationships, gaps, and barriers.

The paper’s public abstract describes a progression from women’s roles and financial transactions to gaps in the existing ecosystem, then to challenges and opportunities for mobile money. Migration history helped explain differences in financial behavior.

The ecosystem view connected a person to the family, community, providers, mobile access, and urban context around each transaction. It created a richer unit of analysis than the transaction screen.

03 / Opportunity

Let context define the product paradigm.

The population’s relationships, risk, access, and constraints defined a distinct product model. Trust, control, household roles, migration experience, and the wider network through which money moved shaped the opportunity space.

The design contribution was a way to move from lived financial behavior to service opportunities while keeping social context visible.

Research sequence

Keep the social system attached to the design question.

The published frame offers a durable sequence for research in complex service environments.

  1. Study lived behavior

    Understand transactions, responsibilities, workarounds, constraints, and existing sources of support.

  2. Map the ecosystem

    Connect household roles to providers, trust networks, mobile access, and migration history.

  3. Identify gaps and barriers

    Locate where the current system creates friction, exclusion, risk, or dependence.

  4. Frame product opportunities

    Develop service paradigms grounded in the conditions the research revealed.

Inclusion changes what a product needs to be.

A fitting service reflects how people make decisions, whom they trust, what risks they carry, and which parts of the current system already help them succeed. That principle continued through my later work in telecom journeys, civic participation, and complex product ecosystems.
