Researching urban wayfinding in the field.

My 2010 HFI role record describes research for Nokia on mobile wayfinding and navigation among low-tech users in Indian cities. I planned recruitment, coordinated and moderated fieldwork, combined interviews with observation, and analyzed emerging evidence with product designers.

The research principle

Wayfinding is a relationship among people, place, information, memory, landmarks, social help, and mobile tools.

Understanding the product opportunity required research in the city with the people it aimed to serve.

Field practice

Several methods, one urban behavior.

Interviews surfaced remembered difficulties and existing strategies. Focus groups revealed shared language and social practices. Fly-on-the-wall observation showed the ordinary actions that participants might leave out of a retrospective account.

Plan

Frame the opportunity and recruit for the right level of mobile familiarity.

Observe

Follow real navigation behavior within the physical and social environment.

Interpret

Analyze emerging evidence with product designers while the team remained in the field.

Develop

Turn patterns into opportunity areas for further product exploration.

Why this is a vignette

The role is detailed. The findings record is missing.

The résumé entry is contemporaneous and unusually specific about the project goal, methods, and responsibilities. HFI’s public client list names Nokia, and an HFI CHI paper identifies Nokia Research among its research collaborations.

An exhaustive local sweep found no Nokia-labeled protocol, screener, field record, analysis file, or final presentation. This page therefore describes the research practice and leaves participants, cities, findings, product decisions, and outcomes open.

What the record shows

A mature field-research loop.

The surviving account captures a close relationship among study design, real-world observation, and product interpretation.

  1. Recruit for the question

    Match participants’ mobile familiarity and urban navigation experience to the opportunity.

  2. Use complementary methods

    Combine individual accounts, group practice, and observed behavior.

  3. Analyze during fieldwork

    Let emerging patterns shape the team’s next questions and observations.

  4. Work with product designers

    Connect the evidence behind each opportunity directly to product thinking.

The city is part of the interface.

People navigate through partial information, local knowledge, social help, habit, language, landmarks, and the physical environment. A mobile product enters that living system. The Nokia work strengthened my practice of bringing designers close to field interpretation.
